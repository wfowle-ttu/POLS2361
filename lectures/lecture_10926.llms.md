# Why Leaders Go To War (Cont.)

1.  In-Out-Group Mechanism
    - Only works when the group is small and has a pre-existing cohesion.
    - If a society is already highly bipolar then these hypothesus are not likely to hold.
2.  Rally Effects
    - These effects are small and short lived
    - E.g. in previous lecture Argentinian president popularity during Falklands War.
    - In some cases there are exceptions like Winston Churcill during WWII, but that required a massive external threat.
    - George Bush’s initial victory in Iraq gave him a boost but that boost was so small it did not even help him achieve re-election.
    - Another criticism of this mechanism is that the public is not naive, they question why leaders go to war, they realize that leaders may initiate wars because they are desperate for popularity.
    - The positive effects of rallying are smaller just before elections.
3.  Scapegoat mechanism
    - The ability to blame domestic problems on external threats
    - It’s hard to associate all domestic issues with external threats.
4.  Gambling of Resurrection
    - This can be argued to be the worst position a leader can be in. For a leader to want to gamble on starting a war to potentially win re-election is a very desperate mechanism. This mechanism does not in many ways help a leader in terms of gaining electoral victory.
    - Costly wars can also inflict punishment and further negative outcomes rather than a higher chance of re-election.

Each of these four mechanisms have significant shortcomings and lack significant empirical findings.

**Empirical Tests for Diversionary Mechanism**

In order to test for this there must be specific domestic patterns: 1. Low Leader Approval 2. Economic Crisis With those issues in place studies look at the correlation between those domestic issues and a state’s propensity to go to war.

Studies have found that there is positive correlation between states experiencing these issues and therefore can said to prove to some degree that there is evidence of diversionary theory.

These problems cannot be said to cause conflict or war, meaning that it is difficult to cause causation between those domestic patterns and the probability of a state to initate/engage in conflict.

Many of these studies have focused on the United States because approval ratings on US presidents are widely available. The US is also one of the only states that can freely initiate conflict without significant costs. This is called *major power bias*. There was strong evidence with regards to the US in terms of domestic conflicts and positive correlation with external conflict. But when smaller powers like the UK or Israel were studied the same evidence was not found.

- Strategic Conflict Avoidance

Targets of conflict tend to avoid conflicts against unpopular leaders because they know they have some incentive to engage in conflict. By this logic other countries should be less likely to go to war against the US during election periods. The enemy would want to avoid the rally affects of attacking a state.

Wars that the US has fought in tend to be initiated early on during a US president’s tenure, not near election periods.

In the US the president enjoys a ‘honeymoon period’ where the presidents approval rating is positive early on during his term. (Usually around the first six months). Most conflicts or war occur during the early period. The enemy specifically does not want to provide any political benefit to the US. This supports the logic of strategic conflict avoidance.

But this means that if you are an unpopular leader and you know the enemy would want to avoid conflict this means you could theoretically levy huge demands. The target therefore would want to avoid conflict but this is where the theory breaks down as to some degree super unreasonable demands could still incentivize the opposing nation to want to engage in conflict.
